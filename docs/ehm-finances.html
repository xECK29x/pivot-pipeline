Version 0.2, 20 November 2026. For database editors and players who want to know what the finance settings actually do. Written from tests on EHM 1.5.0 (build 1669120) with the Pivot database (pre-November release version).

How to read this

Every statement carries one of three labels.

LabelMeaning
TestedWe changed a value or ran a simulation and read the result from the game's saves and screens.
From the game's codeRead from the game's own program logic. It has not been separately confirmed by a simulation.
Not knownWe looked and could not find the rule.

All money is yearly unless it says weekly. The game stores contract wages weekly, so a 1,500 salary becomes 28 a week and shows as 1,456 in some places.

1. The short version

  • A club's Professional status decides which finance rules apply to it at all. An Amateur club has a wage budget of zero and its board will not approve a paid contract.
  • Financial Info rows hold the wage curves and the budget range. Each club uses one row.
  • Wages come from one formula. The spread factor is the exponent in it: a high spread means only high-reputation clubs and people get much above the minimum.
  • The minimum full-time salary is the starting point of every wage, for players and staff alike.
  • A league's minimum and maximum player wage live in its transfer rules, not in Financial Info.
  • TV money is set per league and paid to every club in full. It is the cleanest way to fix a league whose clubs lose money.
  • A league that is not loaded as playable does not run a real economy. Do not judge a league's finances from a save where it was in the background.

3. Professional status

This is the first thing to check when a club cannot sign anyone.

StatusWage budgetStaff contractsWhat we saw
ProfessionalFrom the Financial Info budget curveFull-timeNormal behaviour
Semi-ProfessionalFrom the same curve at three-quarters strengthPart-time, with the part-time minimum as the floorNormal behaviour
JuniorJunior salary allowance × 25, whatever the database saysFull-time1,500 allowance gives 37,500
AmateurZeroNone can be paidBoard allows $0

The Junior and Amateur rows of that table are tested. The Professional and Semi-Professional budget rules are from the game's code.

  • Tested: Amateur clubs cannot pay anyone. In one world, every one of 3,160 Amateur clubs had a budget of 0, and across ten weeks not one of them negotiated a signing. A human manager at an Amateur club saw "Your board will allow a salary up to $0" on a staff offer.
  • Tested: changing the status fixes it. Setting the 62 NCAA clubs from Amateur to Junior gave each a budget of 37,500 on day one, with the database budget left at 0. The board then allowed staff offers, and over a full season every club signed staff and made money.
  • Tested: the Junior budget does not hold AI clubs back. Major junior clubs carry payrolls of 150,000 to 300,000 on a 37,500 budget and sign players every year. The finance screen compares the Player Budget with player salaries only, and junior players earn very little.
  • From the game's code: an Amateur club's wage offer is computed as 0 before any Financial Info value is looked at. So no Financial Info setting can rescue an Amateur club.
  • From the game's code: the statuses None, National (Senior) and National (U20) are not valid for a club that plays in a league.

College leagues are a special case inside the game. A college player's current season shows as "N/A" on the contract screen whatever the club's status, and college clubs receive a yearly intake of new players in late August. Tested: both still happen with the clubs set to Junior. The intake was 17 to 20 years old, and rosters still ran up to age 24.

4. Which Financial Info row a club uses

From the game's code. The game walks the Financial Info table from the top and takes the first row that fits:

  1. a row whose league is the club's league;
  2. otherwise a nation-only row for the nation of the club's league;
  3. otherwise a nation-only row for the club's own nation;
  4. otherwise the first row, which has no nation or league and acts as the default.

Things that follow from that:

  • Tested: you do not need to worry about the order of rows in the Editor or the file. When the game builds a new world it re-orders the table itself: the default row first, then every league row, then the nation rows. A league row therefore always beats its nation's row, wherever it sits in the database file.
  • A nation with only one row uses it for every league in that nation, top flight and third tier alike. Clubs then differ only through their own reputation.
  • Tested: the NCAA has its own row in the Pivot database and does not fall back to the United States row.

5. The wage formula

Almost everything in Financial Info feeds this one formula.

wage = minimum full-time salary + (maximum − minimum full-time salary) × (reputation ÷ 10,000) ^ spread factor

  • maximum is the Max salary for the job or position.
  • reputation runs from 0 to 10,000.
  • spread factor is the Player spread factor for players and the Non-player spread factor for staff.

Whose reputation is used depends on the question:

QuestionReputation used
What will this club pay? (board limit, and staff wages when a game is created)The club's reputation
What does this person ask for?The person's own reputation, blended with ability for players

So a club can afford a person when the club's standing is at least as good as the person's. The two numbers come off the same curve, which is why changing a row moves both the board's limit and the candidates' asks.

Tested: when a game is created, staff wages follow this formula using the club's reputation. For physios and other minor staff it matched to the dollar at every reputation level we checked. For coaches and head coaches it matched at lower reputations and came in under the formula at higher ones, so treat the formula as an upper limit there. What trims the top end is not known.

What the spread factor does

The spread factor decides how much of the gap between minimum and maximum a given reputation earns.

ReputationSpread 2Spread 3Spread 4Spread 5
2,0004.0%0.8%0.16%0.03%
3,50012.3%4.3%1.5%0.5%

10,000100%100%100%100%
  • Low spread: wages rise early. Mid-reputation clubs pay well above the minimum.
  • High spread: wages stay on the floor until reputation is high, then climb steeply.
  • A spread of 4 or more puts every club below about 3,000 reputation on the minimum, however large the maximum is.

Tested example, Great Britain row (head coach maximum 625,000, minimum 10,000):

ClubReputationBoard limit at spread 4.0Board limit at spread 3.0
Second-tier club2,00010,00013,500

At spread 4.0 a head coach asking 11,000 could not be signed. At 3.0 the same club's limit rose to 13,500 and the offer went through. The candidate's ask rose as well, from 10,000 to 12,000, because asks use the same row.

Tested example, NCAA row. With a head coach maximum of 4,000,000 and spread 2.25, college head coaches started on 106,000 to 250,000 and club payrolls were around 420,000. With the maximum at 750,000 and spread 3.5 they started on 21,000 to 56,000. The maximum and the spread together set the level, so change them as a pair.

7. The club wage budget

From the game's code. For a Professional or Semi-Professional club:

budget = min salary budget + (max − min salary budget) × (club strength ÷ 200) ^ salary budget spread factor

  • club strength starts from the club's reputation ÷ 50 and is scaled by the chairman's resources, the attraction of the club's city, its market size and its financial state.
  • The result is rounded to 10,000 and kept above 20 × the minimum full-time salary (20 × the part-time minimum for a Semi-Professional club).
  • In a league with a salary cap, the budget is also tied to the cap.

Tested:

  • What the finance screen compares. "Player Budget" is set against "Total Player Salaries". Staff wages are not counted.
  • Day one. With Starting Financial Adjustments left on, the game changes the database budgets straight away. One league authored at 920,000 for every club started at 700,000 to 920,000; another authored at 690,000 started at 410,000 to 530,000.
  • Every season rollover the budget is set again, and it follows the club's money. In one season we saw rises of 11% to 35% at profitable clubs, a 10% cut at clubs that finished in the red, and every Junior club go from 37,500 to 43,125.
  • Budgets do not strictly limit AI clubs. Clubs commonly run payrolls somewhat over budget.
  • A league that loses money shrinks. Where every club finished in the red, the game cut budgets by 10% at each rollover, season after season. Once the league's income was fixed, budgets stopped moving.

8. Player wages

Starting wages

  • Tick "Disable Starting Wage Adjustments" if you want the database's salaries respected.
  • The option does nothing for players without a salary. If a league's players have none, most of them start on the minimum.
  • Tested: even with the option ticked, a database salary above the league's maximum wage is cut on day one. In our database that explained every remaining change: the leagues with salaries above their maximum were the leagues where salaries dropped, in the same numbers.

League limits

These are in the league's transfer rules, not in Financial Info.

RuleEffectLabel
Contract player min yearly wageNo player in the league is paid less. A lower database salary is lifted to it. Applies to players only, never staffTested for the level; rest from the game's code
Contract player max yearly wageCeiling on any player contract. This is the figure the board quotes for a player offerTested
Contract max yearsLongest contractFrom the game's code
  • Tested: the board allowed a player offer up to exactly the league maximum (40,000 in one league, 30,000 in another).
  • A high minimum wage is expensive. In one second-tier league 71% of players sat on a 35,000 minimum. With rosters growing from 23 to 31 during the season, the minimum alone set most of the wage bill.
  • A league with the minimum and maximum set to the same number has exactly one legal wage. That works: junior and college leagues are set up this way.

Asks

From the game's code. A player's ask comes from the wage formula using a blend of his reputations and current ability, then:

  • is reduced for very high asks and for players aged 34 and over;
  • is multiplied by the foreign player boost if he is foreign;
  • is capped at the league maximum and floored at the league minimum.

9. Staff wages

  • Tested: staff normally have no salary in the database, so the game sets every staff wage when the game is created, from the club's reputation and its Financial Info row (section 5).
  • Tested: the board's limit for a staff offer comes from the same calculation. At a low-reputation club with a steep spread factor, the limit is the minimum full-time salary and nothing more.
  • From the game's code: league minimum wage rules do not apply to staff. The floor for staff is the minimum full-time salary, or the part-time minimum at a Semi-Professional club.
  • A Semi-Professional club offers staff part-time contracts.

If users report that they cannot hire staff, check in this order: the club's Professional status, then the row's Non-player spread factor, then the staff maximums.

The year-end charge

Tested. At the end of each season the game takes a large one-off amount from clubs with a healthy bank balance. It tracks the balance, not that season's profit:

  • about half the balance in most leagues;
  • about 80% in one top European league and about 90% in the NHL.

It behaves like owners taking out accumulated cash. A club can show a "loss" for the year because of it while being profitable on its operations. The exact rule, and the threshold above which it applies, are not known.

11. TV money and prize money

Set per league under Comp Rules, League Rules.

  • Tested: "Total TV money" is paid to every club in full. It is not a pot that is shared out. A league set to 4,000,000 pays each of its clubs 4,000,000.
  • Tested: it is paid on the first day of the season and again at each season rollover.
  • Tested: changing it changes each club's income by exactly that amount in the first season. Six leagues whose clubs lost money were moved to break-even or a small profit by this alone.
  • North American leagues store 0 here and the game works out its own figure per club. The rule is not known.
  • "Total prize money" is a separate pot divided by the prize money type. We have not tested it.

Tested over two seasons. We raised TV money in seven leagues whose clubs were losing money and followed each for two full seasons:

OutcomeLeaguesWhat happened
Fixed5Most or all clubs in profit in both seasons; budgets stable at the rollover
Fixed on average, split underneath1The stronger clubs profit and the three weakest still lose, because all clubs carry similar wage bills
Needed more than TV money1Clubs spent part of the increase; it also needed a lower league minimum wage

In the league that had been losing most consistently, every club was in the red after one season with no TV money and had its budget cut 10% a year. With a modest TV payment every club was profitable by the second season and budgets held steady.

A caution. Extra income can raise the club's budget at the next rollover (section 7), and clubs then spend more. In one league the clubs spent about a quarter of a large TV increase on wages within the first season. Judge a change over two seasons, not one. A flat payment also helps rich and poor clubs alike, so it cannot fix a league where only the smallest clubs lose money.

12. Playable and background leagues

Tested. The game runs a full economy only for leagues loaded as playable.

League stateWhat the game runs
PlayableEvery income and expenditure line
BackgroundNo wage lines and almost no income, but some costs are still charged
Not simulatedA small charge only

So in a long save, the clubs of background leagues drift deep into the red. That is how the game handles leagues it is not simulating in detail. Two leagues that looked ruinous as background leagues were comfortably profitable when loaded as playable.

Only judge a league's finances from a save in which it was playable.

15. What we still do not know

  • What trims coach and head coach wages below the formula at higher-reputation clubs.
  • The rule and threshold for the year-end charge.
  • How the game sets TV revenue for North American leagues.
  • What drives the "Other" income line.
  • Why a handful of NHL clubs take a very large arena charge late in the season.
  • How far into the red a club must be before its board stops a human manager signing people.
  • Whether the Starting Financial Adjustments option keeps an authored budget through later seasons.

16. How this was tested

  • Several new games were started from the same database with one setting changed each time, and the saves were read on day one, monthly, and after each season rollover.
  • Club balances, budgets, contracts and finance lines were read directly from the save files and checked against the in-game finance and contract screens.
  • One ten-year save and three two-season saves with the leagues in question playable supplied the long-run figures.
  • Statements labelled "From the game's code" come from reading the game's program logic for build 1669120. Other builds may differ.
